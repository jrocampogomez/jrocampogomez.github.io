Undergraduate in physics at Stanford, working at KIPAC on the luminosity functions and cosmological evolution of blazars. About

Stanford · KIPAC

I’m a physics major at Stanford University and a research student at the Kavli Institute for Particle Astrophysics and Cosmology, where I work with Professor Vahé Petrosian on the evolution of active galactic nuclei.

I started at Los Angeles City College, did my first research there through the NASA California Space Grant Consortium, and transferred to Stanford as a Jack Kent Cooke Foundation Transfer Scholar. That route shaped what I care about: I like problems where the difficulty is honest and where the method has to be defended line by line, and I have a low tolerance for results that only hold if you don’t look at how the sample was built.

The tools I keep coming back to are nonparametric ones — Efron–Petrosian’s permutation test, Lynden-Bell’s C− method, bootstrap uncertainties — because they let you separate luminosity evolution from selection without assuming the answer first. Long term, I want to work on high-energy astrophysics and on the statistics that make survey data trustworthy.

Outside the lab I write poetry and short fiction, play guitar badly and often, and run telescopes for the public on clear nights.

Luminosity evolution of blazars in γ-rays and radioActive

KIPAC, Stanford University  ·  Advisor: Prof. Vahé Petrosian  ·  2025–present
Methods: Efron–Petrosian permutation test · Lynden-Bell C− · K-corrections · bootstrap

Flat-spectrum radio quasars are detected in two very different surveys at once, and the two selections truncate the sample in different places. I’m measuring how γ-ray and radio luminosity evolve with redshift for a cross-matched 4LAC × VLBI × Gaia sample, using nonparametric methods that handle a doubly truncated dataset without assuming a functional form for the luminosity function.

The work replicates and extends Zeng, Petrosian & Yi (2021): reconstructing local-frame cumulative luminosity functions properly requires de-evolving both the luminosities and the flux limits, which turns out to be where most published treatments quietly go wrong. I rebuilt the analysis pipeline from the ground up, audited the inherited code, and documented each correction.

Manuscript in preparation. Results pending advisor review, so nothing quantitative is posted here yet.

Recovering Fe II reverberation lags in OzDESActive

Simulation study  ·  2026–present  ·  Methods: light-curve simulation · cross-correlation · lag-recovery bias

Reverberation mapping turns a time delay into a black hole mass, so the credibility of the mass depends entirely on whether the lag you recover is the lag that’s really there. I’m building a simulated continuum and broad-line light-curve generator matched to OzDES’s cadence and noise, to characterise how often the Fe II lag is recovered and how it fails when it isn’t.

The first useful output was unglamorous: seven methodological problems in the simulation code I inherited, and a rewritten generator that fixes them.

Blazar evolution pipeline

Python · NumPy · SciPy · Astropy · pandas · Jupyter

An end-to-end analysis of a cross-matched blazar catalogue: sample construction and truncation limits, K-corrections, the Efron–Petrosian test statistic, C− reconstruction of the luminosity function, broken-power-law fitting with multi-start optimisation, bootstrap uncertainties, and a suite of numbered diagnostics that each answer one question about whether the previous step actually worked.

Two fixes I’m fond of: replacing a fragile fsolve call for the maximum accessible redshift with a bracketed brentq solver that cannot silently converge to the wrong root, and catching a sign error in a K-correction average that had been quietly reassigning most of the sample to the wrong subset.

Repository — available on request while the manuscript is in preparation.

Light-curve simulator for reverberation mapping

Python · damped random walk continuum · transfer-function convolution

Generates continuum light curves, convolves them with a chosen transfer function to produce the line response, then re-samples onto a real survey’s cadence with realistic photometric noise so that lag-recovery methods can be tested against a known truth.
