I work on active galaxies — how their light evolves, how big their emitting regions are, and what a survey can’t see.

Physics undergraduate at Stanford and a researcher at KIPAC, splitting my time between the cosmological evolution of blazar luminosity functions and the size of the Fe II emitting region in active galactic nuclei. Both projects come down to the same discipline: every catalogue is missing exactly the objects that would tell you the most, and every measurement is only as good as the method that recovered it.

Currently — finishing an analysis of γ-ray and radio luminosity evolution in flat-spectrum radio quasars. Applying to astrophysics PhD programs this cycle.

About

Stanford · KIPAC

I’m a physics major at Stanford University and an undergraduate researcher at the Kavli Institute for Particle Astrophysics and Cosmology, working with Professor Vahé Petrosian on AGN luminosity evolution and Professor Steven Allen on AGN reverberation mapping.

I started at Los Angeles City College, did my first research there through the NASA California Space Grant Consortium, and transferred to Stanford as a Jack Kent Cooke Foundation Transfer Scholar. That route shaped what I care about: I like problems where the difficulty is honest and the method has to be defended line by line, and I have little patience for results that only hold if you don’t look closely at how the sample was built.

The tools I keep returning to are nonparametric ones — the Efron–Petrosian permutation test, Lynden-Bell’s C− estimator, bootstrap uncertainties — because they separate evolution from selection without assuming the answer first. Alongside the astrophysics I do policy research on space law and nuclear nonproliferation with the Stanford Student Space Initiative. Long term I want to work on high-energy astrophysics and on the statistics that make survey data trustworthy.

Outside the lab I write poetry and short fiction, play guitar badly and often, and run telescopes for the public on clear nights — in English and in Spanish.

Cosmological evolution of FSRQ luminosity functionsActive

KIPAC, Stanford University  ·  Advisor: Prof. Vahé Petrosian  ·  June 2025–present
Methods: Efron–Petrosian permutation test · Lynden-Bell C− · K-corrections · bootstrap

A flat-spectrum radio quasar makes it into this sample only if it is bright enough in γ-rays, bright enough in radio, and has a measured optical redshift — three separate truncations acting on one dataset. I’m determining how the γ-ray and radio luminosity functions of FSRQs evolve with redshift using a tri-variate sample built from Fermi-LAT, VLBI, and Gaia DR2 observations, with nonparametric methods that assume no functional form for the luminosity function and that account for the extra selection bias the optical redshift requirement introduces.

Once evolution is removed, the same machinery yields the intrinsic γ-ray–radio luminosity correlation, which constrains how jet emission relates to emission from the accretion disk. Getting there meant rebuilding the analysis pipeline and documenting every correction: de-evolving both the luminosities and the flux limits is the step that is easiest to get wrong.

Manuscript in preparation. Nothing quantitative is posted here until it has been reviewed.

The Fe II emitting region in AGN, from OzDES reverberation mappingActive

KIPAC, Stanford University  ·  Advisor: Prof. Steven Allen  ·  June 2025–present
Methods: PyCCF · JAVELIN · PyROA · statistical simulation

Reverberation mapping converts a time delay into a physical size, so a radius or a black hole mass is only as trustworthy as the lag it came from. I’m measuring continuum–Fe II line lags in multi-epoch OzDES spectroscopy and testing whether three independent lag estimators agree on the same objects.

Where they disagree, simulations are what tell you which to believe, so a large part of the work is characterising how each method fails and propagating that honestly into the uncertainties. The goal is a constraint on the Fe II radius–luminosity relation that holds up under that scrutiny.

FSRQ luminosity-function pipeline

Python · NumPy · SciPy · Astropy · pandas · Jupyter

End-to-end analysis of a cross-matched blazar catalogue: sample construction and truncation limits, K-corrections, the Efron–Petrosian test statistic, C− reconstruction of the local luminosity function, broken-power-law fitting with multi-start optimisation, bootstrap uncertainties, and a suite of numbered diagnostics that each answer one question about whether the previous step actually worked.

Two fixes I’m fond of: replacing a fragile fsolve call for the maximum accessible redshift with a bracketed brentq solver that cannot silently converge to the wrong root, and catching a sign error in a K-correction average that had been quietly assigning much of the sample to the wrong subset.

Bayesian AGN SED modelling

Python · Metropolis–Hastings MCMC  ·  Stanford Physics 113, 2026

A Python implementation of thin-disk spectral energy distribution fitting for AGN, using Metropolis–Hastings MCMC to infer black hole masses and accretion rates from observational data. It reproduced published measurements for high-redshift blazars, and I used it to test how sensitive the inferred parameters are to the choice of numerical integration scheme — which turned out to matter more than I expected.

Lag-recovery testing for reverberation mapping

Python · PyCCF · JAVELIN · PyROA · simulated light curves

Tooling to run three lag estimators over the same OzDES light curves and compare them, plus a simulator that generates continuum and line light curves on a real survey’s cadence and noise so that recovery can be tested against a known truth.
